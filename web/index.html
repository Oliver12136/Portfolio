To Designing from/for/with Margins

What is it?

These are snapshots of my everyday experiences of exclusion through digital identity verification.

In China, many apps require real-name verification, yet the middle dot (·) in my legal Uyghur name is sometimes rejected as an “illegal character.” Something as simple as signing up for an app can become an absurd negotiation with my own identity.

Why would it happen?

1.Political suppression of Uyghurs

This technical exclusion exists within a broader context of political suppression of Uyghur language and identity.

Even when a validation error is unintentional, rigid digital standards can reinforce whose identities are considered normal—and whose are not.

2.Privilege Hazard

In Data Feminism, Catherine D’Ignazio and Lauren Klein describe the privilege hazard: those who experience the least friction in a system are often least able to recognize whom it excludes.

In my case, simply allowing one additional character in a name field could remove a barrier across many everyday services.

3.Designing Beyond the Default

Experiencing exclusion has made me question who gets treated as the “default user” in design.

It shaped my interest in social and participatory design, and taught me to pay attention to people and experiences that are often overlooked.

I believe people shouldn't have to change who they are to fit into a system. We should design systems that make room for them.

Veritas - (a)live workspace

Why this direction made sense

From “Active Interview” with 10 experienced agents
in 2 sites across Sweden

After each interview, we recorded one-shot videos with the participants, using the initial mappings as a prompt for them to walk us through and reflect on their work. We then remapped the interview material into visual mappings and initial findings for each participant. These mappings became the basis for analysing their workflows more closely, helping us identify recurring patterns, variations, and key frictions across investigative practice. To better understand how this work unfolds through body, space, and action, we also used full body mockups to reconstruct and reflect on their working processes. Based on these frictions, we developed initial concepts, which were then discussed, tested, and refined through two rounds of concept presentations with agents and Europol, ultimately shaping the design direction of Veritas.

Our analysis of computer vision as a technical material showed that the technology pre-indexes video content before any later interaction takes place, bringing these machine interpretations into use from the very beginning. In other words, what agents encounter is not a system that simply “answers questions” in real time, but an analytical entity that has already organized a substantial amount of knowledge about the video. From this perspective, it made more sense to treat AI not merely as a tool, but as another analytical actor. This shifted the design focus from how investigators could better use the tool to how machine interpretation itself could be better communicated, understood, and negotiated. The word cloud therefore became a natural design expression: it not only presents the objects identified by AI, but also visualizes confidence and inter-object relationships through the size and distance of words. This was important because, in investigative work, meaningful clues are often embedded in relationships rather than in simple frequency of occurrence.

What emerged from the research was not one stable investigative workflow, but many different ways of working shaped by role, case, context, and personal expertise. Even within similar institutional settings, agents combined tools, clues, and judgement differently, suggesting that visual investigation tools are only one part of a broader investigative ecology. This pushed the concept away from a fixed interaction flow and toward flexible integration: a workspace that investigators can shape around their own expertise, rather than a system that asks everyone to work in the same way.

So Veritas is...

Not another AI search tool, but a visual investigation workspace that supports non-linear reasoning, mediates between human expertise and computer vision, and remains flexible to different investigative workflows.

Research Paper & Reflection of this project

(a)live workspace

As a Uyghur, I regularly experience exclusion in everyday digital life.

Our names use a middle dot (·), which many real-name authentication systems in China treat as an illegal character. If I include it, the system rejects my name. If I remove it, it no longer matches my official ID, and verification fails. This happens not only in commercial apps, but also on government and essential public service platforms.

In Chinese passports, our full names are often placed entirely in the given-name field, leaving the surname field blank. As a result, we technically have no recognized surname. In countries like the UAE, some Uyghurs have even been denied entry because of this.

All of this exclusion stems from a simple fact: our naming system is different. The fix is simple too - acknowledge our existence. Sometimes, simply acknowledging marginalized communities is enough to remove unnecessary barriers.
